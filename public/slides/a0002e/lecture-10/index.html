Part 1 · Contingency planning

What is an incident?

An adverse event that could result in a loss of information assets, but does not threaten the viability of the entire organisation.

If it does threaten the whole organisation, it is a disaster.

Part 2 · Business impact analysis

Business impact analysis

Risk management asks

How likely is it?

Threats, vulnerabilities, probability.

A BIA asks

It has happened. What now?

Which business functions stop, what that costs, and how long we can live without them.

Part 2 · Business impact analysis

Step 1: find the critical business processes

  • What does the organisation exist to do?
  • What stops if this process stops? Money, care, safety, reputation?
  • Which laws or contracts set a deadline?
  • Which systems, people and suppliers does the process depend on?
Part 2 · Business impact analysis

How long can it be down?

The system goes down. From this moment the clock is running.

Part 2 · Business impact analysis

How long can it be down?

Recovery point objective (RPO). How much data can we afford to lose? Counted back to the last backup we can use.

Part 2 · Business impact analysis

How long can it be down?

Recovery time objective (RTO). The longest the system may be down before it runs again.

Part 2 · Business impact analysis

How long can it be down?

Work recovery time (WRT). The system runs, but data must be restored, checked and caught up before people can work normally.

Part 2 · Business impact analysis

How long can it be down?

Maximum tolerable downtime (MTD). The total time the business can be without the function. RTO and WRT together must fit inside it.

Part 2 · Business impact analysis

Faster recovery costs more

A short RTO needs standby systems, frequent backups and people on call.

A long RTO is cheap until the incident happens. Then the business pays.

The BIA tells you where the balance is for each function.

Part 3 · The incident response cycle

The incident response cycle

Four phases, in a loop

What you learn after one incident goes back into preparing for the next.

The dashed arrow: during containment you often find new things to analyse.

Phases after NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2, Computer Security Incident Handling Guide.

Part 3 · The incident response cycle

The incident response cycle

Phase 1

Preparation

Before anything happens.

  • An incident response plan with roles and contact lists
  • Backups that are tested, and kept where an attacker cannot reach them
  • Logging and monitoring
  • Exercises, so people know the plan

Phases after NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2, Computer Security Incident Handling Guide.

Part 3 · The incident response cycle

The incident response cycle

Phase 2

Detection and analysis

Is this an incident, and how bad is it?

  • Confirm the incident
  • Classify it: type and severity
  • Document everything and keep evidence
  • Prioritise and report, internally and to authorities

Phases after NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2, Computer Security Incident Handling Guide.

Part 3 · The incident response cycle

The incident response cycle

Phase 3

Containment, eradication and recovery

Stop it, remove it, get back.

  • Contain: stop the spread. Isolate machines, block addresses, disable accounts
  • Eradicate: find the root cause and remove the threat
  • Recover: restore systems in priority order and watch for the attacker coming back

Phases after NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2, Computer Security Incident Handling Guide.

Part 3 · The incident response cycle

The incident response cycle

Phase 4

Post-incident activity

What do we learn?

  • Document what happened and what was done
  • Lessons learned: what worked, what did not
  • Update the plan, and feed it back into preparation

Phases after NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2, Computer Security Incident Handling Guide.
